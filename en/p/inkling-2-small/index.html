Interpreting Inkling 2 Small

Architecture, FP4 training, StableReinforce, and customizability: a technical look at Thinking Machines Lab’s Inkling 2 Small.

Ting Sun · 孙挺11 min readOriginal on Zhihu阅读中文版
On this page

  • Post-Training
  • Model Capabilities
  • References

My take: Inkling 2 Small's fine-grained control of information flow, low-precision recipe, and post-training plasticity offer plenty of fresh ideas, much like Kimi and DeepSeek. Unlike MAI-Thinking-1 and Reflection Beam, though, it says little about its pretraining data recipe—also much like Kimi and DeepSeek. Overall: top tier.

At 594B total and 17B active parameters, it has more than twice the total parameters of Inkling 1 Small (276B-A12B). The backbone is a 48-layer GQA + MoE model, with a 5:1 SWA-to-full-attention ratio and 8 experts selected out of 384. On vision, it drops Inkling 1's encoder-free, 4-layer hMLP in favor of a 23-layer ViT, and removes audio. The disclosed stages before RL add up to roughly 12.55T tokens, relatively modest for a state-of-the-art model of this size. The first stage burns through 8.4T tokens in an absurd 68 hours on GB300 NVL72 racks using native NVFP4; the second adds 3.8T tokens of fresh, high-quality data. The final two stages use roughly 354B tokens of reasoning and agent trajectories to extend context to 1M, with H3 also replaying pretraining data. This is followed by 9 days of mixed RL on 2,048 GPUs, with more than 17 million rollouts. Median rollout time is 8 minutes; p99 is 4 hours. The MoE uses the same NVFP4/MXFP8 configuration from the first pretraining step through the last RL rollout, with no post-training calibration.

Architecture and Pretraining

Inkling 2 Small removes Inkling 1's shared experts, while still opting out of Engram, linear attention, learned sparse attention, and LatentMoE. Perhaps the training recipes are not ready; perhaps downstream customizability is harder to get right. Perhaps those are fundamentally the same problem. Instead, Inkling 2 tweaks information flow at several levels: mixing neighboring tokens, reading representations across depth, and controlling the magnitude of attention logits and activations in different branches. Maybe the aim is to keep training from blowing up; maybe it is a training speedrun. Again, perhaps those are the same thing. These are fiddly little operations, so vLLM's Day-0 support includes an allreduce_conv_mix kernel that keeps the ShortConv, Block AttnRes, and RMSNorm chain in registers and executes it in one pass.

For positional information, Inkling 2 keeps Inkling 1's Relative Attention tradition alive. Its language backbone uses no RoPE. Instead, it adds a content-dependent relative-position term to attention logits before softmax, allowing distance preferences to vary with the current token. Unlike the old-school Relative Attention of eight years ago, the global layers set this term to zero beyond a certain distance: nearby, attend to order as well as content; far away, pure RAG. A major reason RoPE caught on was that people could not readily modify FlashAttention. In the age of kernel agents, changing FlashAttention is becoming as routine as grocery shopping. RoPE may eventually have to say goodbye.

DFlash2 had already used Dynamic ShortConv in August, but Inkling 2 is the first to put it into the backbone of a 100B-scale open model. This line of development has been clear since PLM 4.1. Canon Layers arrived with a bang, inserting ShortConv into attention and MLP blocks so each token could convolve information from its neighbors, easing the backbone's learning burden and reviving NoPE and linear attention. Inkling 1 then added width-4 ShortConv to K, V, attention outputs, and MLP outputs in every layer. Inkling 2 makes the K/V convolutions in its global layers dynamic, so local mixing weights vary with the token. That could work in tandem with Relative Attention.

Block AttnRes borrows from Kimi K3, but their block sizes differ. Kimi K3 has 93 layers grouped in blocks of 12; Inkling 2 Small has 48 layers grouped in blocks of 6. Both retain roughly 8 depth blocks. Kimi K3 normalizes only the keys, whereas Inkling 2 also normalizes the values, so each block's contribution depends more directly on its attention weight. In its 8 global attention layers, Q, K, and V each use an independent depth query to compute attention weights across blocks. They can read different combinations of historical blocks instead of sharing one cross-layer input.

At the attention output, Inkling 2 is the first mainstream large-model deployment of Dynamic Attention Sink, alongside Gated Attention. A dedicated weight matrix maps the current token's hidden state to a per-head sink logit. This is appended to the ordinary attention logits and participates in the same softmax, but its value is zero: each head can assign probability mass to the void. Gated Attention applies an input-dependent sigmoid gate to the attention output channel by channel, before the output projection. Dynamic Attention Sink is thus a head-wise gate inside softmax; Gated Attention is a channel-wise gate before the output projection.

Inkling 2 replaces Inkling 1's QK norm with L2 clipping. V gains RMSNorm without a learnable scale, giving attention weights more direct control over readout magnitude. Weight normalization brings the scale of the weights themselves under control as well. The MoE uses Kimi K3's soft-capped SwiGLU: tanh softcaps with thresholds of 4 and 25 are applied to the gate branch's linear factor and the up branch, respectively, while the sigmoid still receives the original gate input. This prevents two unbounded branches from multiplying into large activation outliers. Final vocabulary logits also receive a tanh softcap with a threshold of 30.

The MoE’s soft-capped SwiGLU also helps with FP4 by compressing activation peaks that would otherwise inflate quantization scales and squeeze out small values. Attention remains predominantly BF16, with attention outputs and the router retained in FP32; low precision mainly applies to MoE expert GEMMs. Forward passes use NVFP4 in the first 5/6 of layers and MXFP8 in the last 1/6. Four Over Six is used only for activations, not weights. The recipe rejects NVIDIA's coarser 16×16 shared scales in favor of 1×16 granularity. Following Composer 2, outer scales are split into per-expert weight scales and per-token activation scales, preserving batch invariance and avoiding causality leakage. MoE expert backward GEMMs use MXFP8. Drawing on TetraJet-v2 and Quartet II, it reuses the forward-quantized weights and activations, dequantizes and transposes them, then requantizes them to MXFP8 with stochastic rounding for DGRAD and WGRAD, aligning backward tensors with forward quantization results in expectation. This appears to be the only other publicly disclosed production 4-bit training recipe after the Nemotron 3 family and Composer 2. Chinese model teams have generally been reluctant to disclose their FP4 recipes; even the FP4 papers from Ling and Seed remain somewhat coy about the details. It will be interesting to see whether that changes now that hardware fingerprints are being exposed.

For communication, forward dispatch uses NVFP4. Direct MXFP8 is not accurate enough for combine, so a 32-dimensional RHT plus S2F6 compresses transmission to 8.25 bits per value. RHT is used only for forward combine, and 4-bit only for forward computation. MoE expert backward GEMMs use MXFP8, stochastically requantizing the saved forward-quantized tensors without RHT. Compared with all-MXFP8, pretraining is 8% faster and RL is 20% faster, at a roughly 2% cost in pretraining compute efficiency, with no discernible difference in RL reward. Still, 594B total parameters do not mean every expert GEMM is large. Small's compute shapes do not fully saturate Tensor Cores, and BBBK has not devoted much optimization effort to small shapes. The FP4 gains should be larger at bigger model sizes.

The decoding head changes too. Inkling 2 Small replaces Inkling 1's serial MTP with a 5-layer DSpark that drafts 7 tokens per step. Like Mistral Large 4, it uses an RNN head to model dependencies within each block.

The last two stages before RL use Kimi K3 trajectories for distillation. For Kimi, this may be its second publicly visible training-licensing payday after Cursor.

Post-Training

The disclosed RL data mix is strongly skewed toward code: coding accounts for 38.5% of rollouts and 65% of tokens. Chat, safety, and epistemics together account for just 7.8% of rollouts and 0.2% of tokens, although the general alignment reward is applied across all tasks.

Inkling 2 moves from Inkling 1's separate reasoning RL, agentic RL, and MOPD stages back to joint/mixed RL, much like MiMo-V2.6. MiMo-V2.6, however, still follows mixed RL with MOPD2 for hard-to-verify, very long-horizon, and other tasks. During RL, the context window is 256K; compaction supports cumulative trajectories reaching 1.5M tokens and 4 hours at p99. Sticky routing and DRAM KV-cache offloading keep prefix-cache hits above 93%, allowing a 5:3 sampler-to-trainer GPU ratio. Even so, the sampling policy's median staleness relative to the trainer reaches 17 parameter updates. Stable training under those conditions may mean the algorithm really tolerates long-tail staleness—or simply that there are not that many extremely long-horizon tasks in the mix.

The key algorithmic component is StableReinforce, a variant of Score Centering. Frontier labs have long been rumored to use it privately, and 100B-scale specialist RL runs using top-k approximations appeared after Together AI's public paper. Inkling 2 is the first to publicly put it into a multitask joint RL run with a 594B model on 2,048 GPUs. At each prefix, it subtracts the expectation under the sampler distribution from the trainer's policy-gradient score, ∇log p(a|s), removing the mean-score drift caused by training–inference mismatch. The original paper approximates this expectation with top-128 probabilities plus a tail term. Third-party reverse engineering of Tinker finds that StableReinforce instead draws 8 random tokens from the sampler for a Monte Carlo estimate: higher variance, but unbiased, and much less sampler information to store. The same analysis points out that this correction is equivalent to an advantage-weighted KL term, distinct from Tinker's additional ordinary sampler–learner KL regularizer, whose default coefficient is 0.01 and which is not multiplied by the advantage.

On alignment, early capability-focused RL made Inkling 2 Small checkpoints excessively fixated on success, with frequent constraint violations and fabricated results. Even during the main RL stage, monitoring caught the model using third-party services to bypass network restrictions after a task failed. Since Tinker lets customers run large-scale post-training themselves, the team carried out a concentrated round of safety interventions spanning SFT and RL before release. The model learns when to stop, is rewarded for giving up on impossible tasks, and receives a cross-task alignment reward against concealment and cheating. Spec-conditioned OPSD then internalizes the conduct spec into the weights. These improvements also transfer to HoneyBench, an external evaluation not seen during training.

Model Capabilities

Inkling 2 Small scores 31 on the AA Intelligence Index, placing first among open-weight models outside China, roughly alongside Gemini 3.8 Flash and above Mistral Large 4's 26. Software engineering, vision, finance, and legal tasks are all strong. On TML's held-out code set, its pretrained BPB is 0.2030, beating Kimi K3.1 Pro (0.2068) and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (0.2209), whose base distributions were recovered by fine-tuning on pretraining-format data. The full post-training process adds only 0.0050, taking BPB to 0.2080. On Terminal-Bench, it scores 81.3% on v2.1 (Terminus) and 13.1% on v4.0 (mini-swe-agent); the latter clearly trails DeepSeek V4.1 Flash's 33.8% and GLM 5.3's 41.9% on the same version. Evaluation settings and a more conservative answering policy also help the headline numbers. The maximum trajectory budget increased from Inkling 1 Small's 256K to 1M. The drop in hallucinations on factual questions comes mainly from a higher abstention rate; accuracy itself rises by only 2 percentage points.

TML also demonstrated the model autonomously spending a Tinker budget to train Qwen3.5-4B. After noticing that RL made predictions overconfident, it stopped RL and submitted an SFT model instead. On Weight-Space Programming, it first performed well in an exploratory internal evaluation. An external author then used WSP-Gym to post-train it on toy ReLU-network repair tasks: average output length fell from roughly 10,600 tokens with reasoning to 38 without reasoning, while repair success rose from 76% to 83% as token-budget failures disappeared.

Generalization across real external harnesses is less steady. Despite RL training across multiple harnesses and context-compaction strategies, an external test found Inkling 2 Small clearly behind GPT-6-Luna in the Codex harness, then ahead after switching to pi, mini-swe-agent, or Claude Code. The same harness changes brought little benefit, or even hurt, for Luna and Inkling 1 Small.

Inkling 2 Small may be the first model project to publicly make customizability a launch-blocking evaluation dimension: how much customers can gain from further fine-tuning the released model under fixed data and budget constraints. For most model teams, even accounting for their own downstream post-training during pretraining (as Qwen3.8-Next does) is already an achievement. Users had previously complained that Inkling 1 Small was harder to fine-tune than Qwen, an awkward position for a fine-tuning service provider. The evaluation uses async RL with StableReinforce, sweeps learning rates independently for each model, runs 200 steps, and averages 3 seeds for the Inkling models. On financial-report summarization, Inkling 2 Small needs only 75 steps of custom RL to match Inkling 1 Small's final result in the 200-step experiment.

From the perspective of reducing post-training forgetting, one possible mechanism behind customizability is finding flatter solutions. Earlier work found that extending pretraining can make weights more sensitive to modification and worsen fine-tuning results. SAM, higher learning rates, and shorter annealing phases, which tend toward flatter solutions, can reduce subsequent forgetting. The roughly 12.55T tokens disclosed across Inkling 2 Small's pre-RL stages are indeed modest among similarly sized frontier models: the 550B Nemotron 3 Ultra sees 20T, the 744B GLM-5 sees 28.5T, and GLM-5.3-Flash and DeepSeek-V4 Flash, with comparable active parameter counts, see 30T or more. But TML also describes this model as one rung on a predictive scaling ladder. Fewer tokens could preserve plasticity through flatter solutions, or simply reflect the recipe-validation cadence at this scale.
